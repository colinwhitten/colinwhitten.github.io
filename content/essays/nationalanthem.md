The Star Spangled Banner is one of the worst songs of all time. The lyrics don't mean anything and they're about a flag still standing after a long battle in a war that nobody cares about. The Star Spangled Banner is only made relevant as a cultural artifact in the way it's been perverted by creative and ambitious renditions. Perhaps most famously, at the Woodstock music festival in 1969 Jimi Hendrix played the anthem in such a way that he used gain and distortion on his guitar to mimic the sound of rockets and bombs. Fergie's rendition of the national anthem at the 2018 NBA All Star game was likened by critics to a "jazzy, sexed-up" version of the anthem. This song has been around since 1814 and has only _really_ mattered twice: at Woodstock when a guitar sounded like bombs and at the NBA All Star Game in 2018. It's a dumb song that doesn't even sound good and it doesn't really mean anything at all.

What I would like to propose is not only a new national anthem for our nation but a new philosophy for what national anthems should mean. First, there are basically only two types of countries: you are either Europe, or you at some point gained independence from Europe. If you belong to the first category, you ought to keep your national anthem as is. These countries already have long, storied traditions of classical music to which their national anthems pay homage. That's great! If any of these countries had exported anything culturally relevant in the last four hundred years, I'd argue for updating their national anthems. But they have not! The only exceptions I'd make are the U.K. and France. For England I'd suggest updating their national anthem to be Eleanor Rigby and for France, something by Serge Gainsbourg or Stromae.

The second kind of country is one that has gained independence from Europe, and I understand the urge upon gaining independence to choose a national anthem which conforms to traditional conventions of European, classical-style music. I understand this in the context of making of an effort to establish some sense of recognition and respect on a global stage as a new country. It makes sense but what cultural tradition does this pay homage to? If your country has a globally recognizable sound, wouldn't it be nice for the anthem to sound like that? Easily the best example being Jamaica, would it not make sense for their anthem to sound like reggae? Nigeria ought to have their anthem incorporate some elements of Afrobeats; Brazil could include some elements of Samba and Bossa Nova in their anthem, Cuba's new national anthem could be Chan Chan.

The United States is huge and diverse as a nation could be, but not too big and diverse to preclude us from trying to ascribe a singular sound to define what America means as a country. I'd like to suggest two options for new American national anthems: one for the country we _could/should_ be, and one for the country we _really are._

There really is an alternate timeline in which America wholeheartedly embraces the cultures built by its most marginalized people, the same cultures which eventually become America's most prized cultural exports. That's not the America we live in!

IF we did: our national anthem would be "Ornithology" by Charlie Parker. Parker was the de facto leading performer and composer of jazz's bebop era. Bebop jazz, a style which I will argue represents what America is, at its best. It was developed in the early 1940s in New York City by a group of younger musicians, led by Parker, and including other icons like Miles Davis, Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, and Max Roach. Bebop expanded what was possible in jazz and popular music altogether. It was a type of music defined by breaking rules: fast tempo, chord changes, changes of key, complex chord progressions, and perhaps most importantly, _improvisation!_ In our grandest and most ambitions of what our country is, we imagine our country to be a place where, if you can dream it, you build it. If the rules don't work for you, bend them or break them until they work in your favor. Charlie Parker, for better or worse, is a great mascot for this version of America. Brash and imperfect but wildly talented, he represented the jazz subculture as not just a musician or entertainer but as an uncompromising artist and intellectual. And I think when we dig deep into the American psyche and think about what we imagine ourselves as, we have this tendency as Americans to connect with the antihero. Francis Scott Key, one one hand, was born into a wealthy New England family and went on to become a prolific slave-owning lawyer. In Charlie Parker, we are able to reimagine America in the image of the flawed but profoundly talented individual: the uncompromising artist who works hard and plays hard. He's a womanizer and famously addicted to heroin; but he also largely invented a subgenre of music which ultimately had tremendous influence on _all_ popular music which came after it. I'd argue we don't get this next song without Charlie Parker.

We don't live in that country though. Could you imagine! Our national anthem being a bebop jazz standard? It sounds too good to be true because it is. Our country still does need to change its national anthem, but I argue we should instead get the national anthem we _deserve._

The national anthem we deserve tells the story of a young woman who vandalizes her boyfriend's car because she suspects him of cheating, Carrie Underwood's 2005 hit "Before He Cheats." It's set in the key of F-sharp minor and has a main chord pattern of F#m-E-Dsus-2-C#. In the song's lyrics, she imagines her boyfriend out with a "bleach-blonde" woman playing pool, dancing, and buying her a drink. And while her boyfriend is out flirting with another woman, she vandalizes his car by keying the sides and carving her name into the leather seats, smashing the headlights with a Louisville Slugger baseball bat and slashing the tires. What makes this song so spiritually American and perfect for our cultural moment is the fact that this act of vandalism is entirely preemptive: the song is called "Before He Cheats," and the narrative of her boyfriend flirting with a young blonde woman at a bar is entirely imagined! It's not a revenge story, but a story of striking first. Maybe it's a metaphor for launching preemptive missile or drone strikes on a sovereign nation because this nation, for example: appears to have the potential for owning weapons of mass destruction, or appears to have the ability to enrich uranium. For Carrie Underwood's "Before He Cheats," she is also launching a preemptive strike on her boyfriend because she is convinced he is cheating. It's a perfect song to capture the spirit of paranoia that has a grip on our cultural identity. We are of course always at war or on the brink of war and this makes us paranoid. But especially in recent years we are also collectively wracked with the anxiety and paranoia that Everything Will Come Crashing Down at some point very very soon. And the answer: smash and grab! Smash your boyfriend's car! Loot and hoard wealth to protect yourself from _whatever_ is supposed to be happening in the near future. Maybe something crazy will happen to make Everything Come Crashing Down. Probably not! But your boyfriend _will_ cheat on you!
