Two and a half years ago I moved out of my home country, from San Francisco to Berlin. I was twenty six years old two and a half years ago. In leaving a cosmopolitan lifestyle on the West Coast in the U.S. to experience what ultimately became an almost identical cosmopolitan experience in a new country, I had convinced myself that by making a move like this I was achieving a Sense of Self! Rachel Cusk’s is taking down the concept of the Character, or so I’m told. 

In Life of M she is writing a novel inspired by the life of Natalie Portman, or “M”. A central theme is that Matalie Mortman, having been an actor and celebrity since about 12 when she had her breakout role in Leon: The Professional, has completely assumed the role of actor not only in her profession but in her personal identity. All of M’s decisions are calculated. It’s like how so many NBA players become media trained since the moment they slam dunk at age eleven, and their personalities are trained out of them. Feeling compassion or empathy for M are cold decisions, “Of all the things she could have chosen, she had selected goodness.” I’m not even going to get into Rachel Cusk’s writing style because I may LOSE MY LUNCH if I revisit this prose too much. 

Where is the deconstruction of the self happening here? Is it because M is a famous celebrity, or because she is specifically a famous actor? Or is it some third thing? There’s a lot in the text that suggests there is perhaps something specific about the intersection of being both famous and an actor by trade that causes this withering away of the character, or self. 

“This meeting in its corporate setting also has its basis in ritual, but it is less clear from where this ritual’s rules and features have come. It is one of the occasions on which M wonders to what degree people have learned to behave in certain situations by imitating what they have seen in films. It is common enough in films to have scenes of this procedural nature: office dramas, boardroom meetings, goings-on in law courts and police or hospital departments. These scenes almost always have a kind of sincerity that rests on the unquestioned belief that they correspond with something that already exists in reality.”

Later on:

“What the film actor struggles with, he [theater director] says, is the notion that the play is more important than they are.” 

Surely the Big Idea here can’t be that because M is acting all the time on screen, she is, when off-screen, “losing track of when she’s supposed to be acting and when she’s supposed to be sincere,” right? And how can you be sincere if being sincere is a deliberate decision you’re making: “Okay, time to be sincere.” 

Of course, another big takeaway from this book is that Natalie Portman, and celebrities like her, are tremendously uninteresting. But we knew that already! We could have gotten this point across in ten or fifteen pages. We do live in their [celebrities’] world, and I guess all of us can absolutely relate to the passage above where the line blurs between having some genuine experience versus having some preconception of what the experience is going to look like based on what we’ve seen of it already from television, film, or the internet. Our brains have become so informed by what we see on the internet that we are increasingly acting, in the real world, how we think we are supposed to act based on what we’ve seen online. 

So yes, well done Rachel Cusk you have convinced me that 1) Celebrities are boring, and 2) there is something about our modern existence that withers away our sense of self. Making this argument by centering the book around a writer and an actor is like playing a game on easy mode. These are easily the two most self-indulgent and self-serious professions out there. And this book is so self indulgent and self serious. It’s so self serious that I think that she’s done an amazing job of proving these points, albeit perhaps on accident!
Question: if not Matalie Mortman or Rachel Cusk, who then, has the strongest case for having a sense of self? Who makes the case in favor of the existence of the Character? Nobody involved in this book, I can tell you that much! And definitely not myself! If you have a nice place in Paris, London, Berlin, San Francisco, or New York, sorry! You’re not a character! Better luck next time!
